   * Gave citizenship to everyone born or naturalized in the us, overturning dred scott

# Thesis Sentence Frames

* Causation
   * [Event/development] was primarily caused by [cause 1] and [cause 2], which led to [effect].
   * Although [cause A] played a role, [cause B] was the most significant cause of [event] because [reason].
   * [Event] led to [effect 1] and [effect 2], as shown by [example 1] and [example 2].
* Continuity and Change (CCOT)
   * From [year] to [year], [topic] changed as [change], although [continuity] stayed the same because [reason].
   * While [topic] changed significantly through [change 1] and [change 2], [continuity] remained because [reason].
* Comparison
   * While both [A] and [B] [similarity], they differed in [difference] because [reason].
   * [A] and [B] were similar in that [similarity], but [A] [difference] whereas [B] [difference].
* Extent / Evaluate
   * To a large extent, [claim], as seen in [category 1] and [category 2], although [limit or counterpoint].
   * Although [counterpoint], [claim] was largely true because [reason 1] and [reason 2].
* Complexity add-on (use with any thesis)
   * …; however, [counterargument], which shows that [more nuanced claim].
   * …, but this change was limited because [group/region] still [continuity].

# Contextualization Frames {Context}

* Before [period/event], [broader development], which set the stage for [topic].
* In the years leading up to [year], [trend/conflict] created tension over [issue], leading to [topic of the prompt].
* [Earlier development] had already [effect], so by [year] Americans were [situation], which explains why [topic].

# Commentary Sentence Frames

* Explaining evidence
   * This shows that [point] because [explanation].
   * This demonstrates how [evidence] [caused/changed/proved] [result], which supports the argument that [thesis claim].
   * This is significant because it led to [effect], which [connects to thesis].
   * As a result, [consequence], which reveals that [larger point about the period].
   * In other words, [restate evidence in your own words], meaning that [analysis].
* Linking back to the thesis
   * Therefore, [evidence] proves that [thesis claim].
   * This connects to the larger pattern of [theme], showing that [claim].
   * By [action], [group] was able to [result], which reflects [thesis idea].
* Sourcing (HIPP: Historical situation, Intended audience, Purpose, Point of view)
   * Because the author was [position/identity], they likely [view/purpose], which affects the document because [reason].
   * The historical situation of [event/context] explains why [author] argued [claim].
   * [Author] was writing to [intended audience], so they [emphasized/left out] [detail] in order to [purpose].
   * The purpose of this document was to [purpose], which helps the argument because it shows [point].
* Complexity
   * However, [counter-evidence], which complicates the argument because [explanation].
   * While [claim] was true for [group/region], it was not true for [other group/region] because [reason].
   * This change was limited, though, since [continuity] still [remained/persisted] because [reason].

# LEQ Structure {Outline}

* Intro paragraph
   * Context: 2–4 sentences on what was happening before or around the prompt's time period, specific (names, dates, events), leading into the topic.
   * Thesis: last sentence of the intro. Answers the prompt, takes a position, and names your two lines of reasoning (claim 1 and claim 2). Add a "although…" or "however…" for complexity.
* Body paragraph 1: Claim 1
   * Claim: topic sentence stating your first reason, in the words of the thesis.
   * Evidence: a specific event, law, person, or date from the period.
   * Commentary: explain how that evidence proves the claim ("This shows that… because…").
   * Evidence: a second specific example, ideally from a different part of the time period.
   * Commentary: explain it, and link it back to the thesis.
* Body paragraph 2: Claim 2
   * Claim: topic sentence stating your second reason.
   * Evidence: a specific example.
   * Commentary: explain how it proves the claim.
   * Evidence: another specific example.
   * Commentary: explain it, and connect it back to the thesis.
* Complexity (optional third body paragraph, or a few sentences)
   * Claim: a counterargument, a continuity next to a change, or a different group or region the claim does not fit.
   * Evidence: an example of it.
   * Commentary: explain why it complicates or qualifies your thesis rather than disproving it.
* Conclusion
   * Restate the thesis in new words and say why it mattered for what came next.
* Rubric points to aim for
   * Thesis (1): a defensible claim with a line of reasoning, in the intro.
   * Contextualization (1): broader context before or during the period, more than a passing phrase.
   * Evidence (2): at least two specific examples, and using them to support the argument.
   * Analysis and reasoning (2): explaining how the evidence proves the thesis using causation, comparison, or continuity and change, plus complexity.
* For a CCOT prompt
   * Body paragraph 1 is the change, body paragraph 2 is the continuity (or two kinds of change), and the commentary should say why things changed or why they stayed the same.

# Sample LEQs {LEQs}

* How to use these
   * The newer prompts cover a longer stretch of time, so pick evidence from both the start and the end of the period, not just one event.
   * Each response here is the core of an essay: thesis, context, two pieces of evidence, and the analysis that ties them to the thesis.
* Prompt 1: Evaluate the extent to which British imperial policy changed the relationship between Britain and its North American colonies from 1651 to 1776.
   * Thesis: Although Britain had regulated colonial trade since the Navigation Acts, its relationship with the colonies changed greatly after 1763, as the end of salutary neglect and new taxes like the Stamp Act turned loyal colonists into rebels who declared independence.
   * Context: Starting with the Virginia Company's founding of Jamestown in 1607 as a joint-stock company, England treated its colonies as sources of profit under mercantilism, the idea that colonies existed to send raw materials to the mother country and buy its manufactured goods. Because England was far away and caught up in its own civil war in the 1640s, colonists built their own governments, like the House of Burgesses (1619) and the Mayflower Compact (1620), and got used to running their own affairs.
   * Evidence 1: The Navigation Acts (1651) required colonial goods to travel on British ships and enumerated goods to go only to England, but under salutary neglect Britain rarely enforced them, so colonists smuggled and traded freely.
   * Evidence 2: After the French and Indian War left Britain in debt, Parliament passed the Proclamation of 1763, the Stamp Act (1765), and the Intolerable Acts (1774), taxing and controlling the colonies directly.
   * Analysis: This shows a real change, because before 1763 the colonists accepted British rule while it stayed loose, but once Britain enforced its power without colonial representation, groups like the Sons of Liberty organized resistance, which led directly to Common Sense and the Declaration of Independence in 1776.
* Prompt 2: Evaluate the extent to which ideas about religion and liberty shaped American society from 1630 to 1800.
   * Thesis: Religious ideas strongly shaped American society from 1630 to 1800, as Puritan efforts to build a "city upon a hill" gave way to dissent and religious tolerance, and the First Great Awakening encouraged colonists to question authority in ways that fed revolutionary ideas of liberty.
   * Context: The Protestant Reformation had split Europe in the 1500s, and in England the Puritans wanted to purify the Church of England of its Catholic practices. Facing persecution under King Charles I, about 20,000 Puritans left in the Great Migration of the 1630s, after the Pilgrims had already sailed on the Mayflower in 1620 to separate from the church completely.
   * Evidence 1: John Winthrop's "City Upon a Hill" (1630) described Massachusetts Bay as a model Puritan community, yet Roger Williams was banished for arguing for separation of church and state and founded Rhode Island (1636) as a haven for religious tolerance.
   * Evidence 2: In the 1730s and 1740s the First Great Awakening, led by George Whitefield and Jonathan Edwards, stressed a personal relationship with God over obedience to church officials, and it appealed to people of every class.
   * Analysis: This mattered beyond religion, because once colonists believed they could challenge church authority, it became easier to challenge political authority too, which helped prepare them to accept the Declaration of Independence's claim that people could overthrow a government that abused its power.
* Prompt 3: Evaluate the extent to which the Constitution resolved the problems facing the United States from 1781 to 1800.
   * Thesis: The Constitution largely fixed the weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation by creating a stronger federal government that could tax and enforce its laws, but it did not settle disagreements over federal power, which split Americans into rival political parties by 1800.
   * Context: In the 1760s and 1770s, Parliament's taxes like the Stamp Act (1765) and punishments like the Intolerable Acts (1774) convinced colonists that a strong central government that ignored them was a threat to liberty, and the Declaration of Independence (1776) said governments get their power from the consent of the governed. So when the Second Continental Congress wrote the Articles of Confederation (ratified 1781), it left power with the 13 states: Congress could not tax, regulate trade, or raise an army on its own, and changing the Articles needed every state to agree.
   * Evidence 1: Shays' Rebellion (1786–1787) showed that the national government under the Articles could not raise an army or deal with debt, which pushed leaders to write a new constitution with compromises like the Great Compromise.
   * Evidence 2: Under the Constitution, Washington used federal troops to stop the Whiskey Rebellion (1794), but Hamilton's Economic Plan and the Alien and Sedition Acts (1798) caused fierce debates over how much power the federal government should have.
   * Analysis: The Whiskey Rebellion shows that the new government could now enforce its laws, which the Articles never could, but the fights over Hamilton's bank and the Alien and Sedition Acts show the problem of federal power was only partly solved, since it produced the party conflict behind Jefferson's "Revolution of 1800."
* Prompt 4: Evaluate the extent to which economic changes transformed American society from 1800 to 1848.
   * Thesis: The Market Revolution transformed American society from 1800 to 1848 by connecting regions through new transportation and creating factory work in the North, while it also deepened the South's dependence on cotton and slavery.
   * Context: In 1800 the large majority of Americans lived on farms, growing food mostly for their families and local markets, and moving goods overland by wagon on dirt roads was slow and expensive. * Prompt 5: Evaluate the extent to which territorial expansion increased sectional tensions in the United States from 1820 to 1861.
   * Thesis: Territorial expansion was the main cause of rising sectional tension from 1820 to 1861, because every new territory reopened the question of whether slavery would spread, and the compromises meant to settle it failed one after another until the South seceded.
   * Context: The Constitution (1787) put off settling slavery through the Three-Fifths Compromise and a 20-year protection of the slave trade, and by 1819 there were 11 free and 11 slave states, so each section had equal votes in the Senate. The Northwest Ordinance (1787) had banned slavery north of the Ohio River, and the Louisiana Purchase (1803) doubled the country's size, raising the question of whether slavery would spread into the new land.
   * Evidence 1: The Missouri Compromise (1820) kept the balance between free and slave states and banned slavery north of 36°30', but the land won in the Mexican-American War led to the Wilmot Proviso and the Compromise of 1850, including a stronger Fugitive Slave Act that angered the North.
   * Evidence 2: The Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) repealed the Missouri Compromise line and used popular sovereignty, which led to violence in Bleeding Kansas, and Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857) ruled that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories.
   * Analysis: Each step west forced the nation to decide the future of slavery, and as compromises collapsed into violence, Northerners and Southerners came to see each other as threats, which is why Lincoln's election in 1860 on a promise to stop slavery's spread led Southern states to secede.
* Prompt 6: Evaluate the extent to which the Civil War and Reconstruction changed the lives of African Americans from 1861 to 1877.
   * Thesis: The Civil War and Reconstruction changed the legal status of African Americans by ending slavery and granting citizenship and voting rights, but these changes were limited because Black Codes, sharecropping, and Klan violence kept many in poverty and without real power by 1877.
   * Context: By 1860 about four million African Americans were enslaved in the South, around a third of its people. Southern slave codes kept them from learning to read, traveling without passes, or testifying against white people, and Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857) ruled that Black Americans, free or enslaved, could not be U.S. citizens. Lincoln's election in 1860 on a promise to stop slavery's spread led 11 Southern states to secede and form the Confederacy.
   * Evidence 1: The Emancipation Proclamation (1863), the 13th Amendment (1865), and the 14th and 15th Amendments ended slavery, made African Americans citizens, and gave Black men the right to vote, with help from the Freedmen's Bureau and its schools.
   * Evidence 2: Southern states passed Black Codes to control freed people's labor, sharecropping trapped many families in debt, and the Ku Klux Klan used terror to stop Black men from voting.
   * Analysis: This shows that change was real on paper but limited in practice, because once the Compromise of 1877 removed federal troops from the South, nothing protected the new rights, and Southern Democrats rolled them back through Jim Crow laws.
* Prompt 7 (CCOT): Evaluate the extent to which slavery in North America changed from 1619 to 1865.
   * Thesis: From 1619 to 1865, slavery changed from a small, loosely defined labor system into a large, race-based institution at the center of the Southern economy and national politics, although the continuity of enslaved people resisting their bondage remained throughout the period until the Civil War and the 13th Amendment ended it.
   * Context: In the 1500s Spain and Portugal had already built the Atlantic slave trade to work sugar plantations in the Caribbean and Brazil, and the Spanish used the encomienda system to force Native Americans to labor. After the Virginia Company founded Jamestown in 1607, the colony survived by growing tobacco, which John Rolfe introduced around 1612 and which needed huge amounts of labor; in 1619, the same year the House of Burgesses first met, the first recorded Africans arrived in Virginia.
   * Evidence 1 (change): In the 1600s Virginia relied mostly on indentured servants, but after Bacon's Rebellion (1676) showed the danger of poor white servants and enslaved people joining together, planters turned to enslaved Africans and passed laws making slavery permanent, inherited, and based on race.
   * Evidence 2 (change): After the cotton gin (1793), cotton became the South's main crop and slavery spread west with it, so that by 1860 nearly four million people were enslaved, and fights over slavery in new territories led to the Missouri Compromise, the Kansas-Nebraska Act, and Dred Scott.
   * Evidence 3 (continuity): Enslaved people resisted throughout the period, from the Stono Rebellion (1739) to Nat Turner's Rebellion (1831), and each time Southern colonies and states answered with harsher slave codes.
   * Analysis: This shows that slavery grew from one labor option into the foundation of the Southern economy, which made Southerners defend it more and more aggressively as the North moved away from it, while the constant resistance of enslaved people shows that the system always depended on force; that growing conflict is why it took a civil war, the Emancipation Proclamation, and the 13th Amendment (1865) to end it.
* Prompt 8 (CCOT): Evaluate the extent to which the institution of slavery and the lives of enslaved and freed African Americans changed from 1800 to 1877.
   * Thesis: From 1800 to 1877, slavery changed from a growing institution protected by national compromises into one destroyed by the Civil War and the 13th Amendment, but the continuity of white Southern efforts to control Black labor and deny Black Americans power remained, as Black Codes, sharecropping, and the end of Reconstruction kept most freed people poor and unequal.
   * Context: The Constitution (1787) protected slavery through the Three-Fifths Compromise, which counted three of every five enslaved people toward a state's representation, a fugitive slave clause, and a ban on ending the slave trade before 1808. After the Revolution, Northern states from Vermont (1777) to New Jersey (1804) began to end slavery, mostly gradually, and the Northwest Ordinance (1787) banned it north of the Ohio River, so by 1800 the nation was already splitting into free and slave sections.
   * Evidence 1 (change, growth): The cotton gin and the Market Revolution made cotton the South's most valuable crop, so slavery spread west into new states, and conflicts over it produced the Missouri Compromise (1820), the Compromise of 1850 with its stronger Fugitive Slave Act, and the Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854).
   * Evidence 2 (change, defense and resistance): After Nat Turner's Rebellion (1831) Southern states passed harsher laws restricting enslaved people, while abolitionists in the North grew louder, and Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857) ruled that Black Americans could not be citizens and Congress could not ban slavery in the territories.
   * Evidence 3 (change, abolition): The Civil War turned into a war to end slavery with the Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and the service of about 180,000 Black soldiers, and the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments abolished slavery, granted citizenship, and gave Black men the vote.
   * Evidence 4 (continuity): After emancipation, Southern states passed Black Codes, many freed people were trapped in debt through sharecropping and the crop-lien system, and the Ku Klux Klan used violence to stop them from voting; after the Compromise of 1877 removed federal troops, Southern Democrats took back control.
   * Analysis: This shows that the legal institution of slavery changed completely, since it went from being protected and expanding to being abolished in the Constitution, which was the biggest change in Black Americans' lives in the period. However, the continuity of white control over Black labor and politics shows how limited that change was, because once the federal government stopped enforcing Reconstruction, many freed people were left in conditions that looked a lot like slavery, which set up the Jim Crow era.
* Prompt 9 (CCOT): Evaluate the extent to which the status and rights of women in the United States changed from 1800 to 1877.
   * Thesis: From 1800 to 1877, women changed their role in public life by organizing reform movements, demanding equal rights at Seneca Falls, and taking on new work during the Civil War, but the continuity of separate spheres and laws that kept women from voting and controlling property meant their legal and political status changed very little.
   * Context: During the Revolution, Abigail Adams asked her husband John to "remember the ladies" (1776), but the new nation kept women out of politics. Under coverture, inherited from English law, a married woman's property, wages, and legal identity belonged to her husband, and the idea of Republican Motherhood said women served the republic by raising virtuous sons rather than by voting; New Jersey, which had let property-owning women vote, took that right away in 1807.
   * Evidence 1 (continuity): The Cult of Domesticity (separate spheres) taught that women belonged in the home while men took part in politics and business, and even the young women who worked in the Lowell textile mills in the 1830s and 1840s were paid less than men and expected to leave work when they married.
   * Evidence 2 (change): Women became leaders in reform movements like temperance and abolition, where the Grimké sisters and Sojourner Truth spoke out, and at the Seneca Falls Convention (1848) Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott issued the Declaration of Sentiments, which said "all men and women are created equal" and demanded the vote.
   * Evidence 3 (change): During the Civil War, women ran farms and businesses, worked as nurses under Dorothea Dix and Clara Barton, and raised money through the U.S. Sanitary Commission, showing they could take on public roles.
   * Evidence 4 (continuity): The 14th Amendment (1868) used the word "male" in the Constitution for the first time, and the 15th Amendment (1870) protected voting from discrimination by race but not by sex, which split the movement into Stanton and Susan B. Anthony's National Woman Suffrage Association and Lucy Stone's American Woman Suffrage Association (1869).
   * Analysis: This shows that women's place in public life changed a lot, since reform work, Seneca Falls, and the war gave them experience organizing and speaking for themselves, which built the movement that would later win the 19th Amendment. However, their legal and political status stayed mostly the same, because by 1877 women still could not vote in national elections and most married women still had limited property rights, showing that the expansion of rights during Reconstruction left women out.
